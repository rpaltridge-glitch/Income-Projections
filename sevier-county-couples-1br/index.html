What separates them

Four things the measurement shows, each one computed across all 186 cabins rather than picked from the leaders.

Rate is the lever, and it is nearly free

Sorted into five bands by nightly rate, the cheapest fifth holds $208 and grosses $56,392; the dearest fifth holds $332 and grosses $87,240. Occupancy barely moves across that whole ladder — 78% down to 70%. A 60% higher rate costs about 9 points of occupancy and returns 55% more revenue.

Sleeping two costs nothing

The 85 cabins that sleep two median $67,801. The 94 that sleep four median $63,559. Building for two guests rather than four does not reduce the ceiling in this market — the highest-grossing cabin in the county sleeps two.

A hot tub is table stakes; a theater room is not

180 of 186 have a hot tub, so it cannot be a differentiator — it is the price of entry. The 21 with a theater or cinema room median $83,277, and 8 of the top twenty have one. An indoor or private pool is worth less than it looks: $70,943 against $64,134.

This is not just older listings

Cabins with fewer than 50 reviews median $63,728 against $70,149 for those with 300 or more. Review history helps, but it does not explain the gap between a $56,392 year and a $87,240 one. A new build is not locked out of the top tier.

Architecture and amenities — what the top producers share

Every amenity in the data and every architectural term in the listing copy was tested against all 186 cabins. Two things came out of it, and the first is the more surprising.

Architectural style does not predict revenue. Modern and contemporary cabins median $65,578 against $66,489 for the market — no reliable difference, and the gap does not survive controlling for nightly rate. A-frames, treehouses, barns and domes are all too few to call. What does show up is a penalty at the other end: the 60 cabins whose copy leans on rustic or log-cabin styling median $60,232 against $68,036 for everything else. The lesson for a new build is not “pick a style” — it is that the dated Smokies log-cabin look is the one identifiable architectural drag in this segment.

The six counted are: a theater or cinema room · a sauna · a sound system or record player · a skylight or star-gazing feature · an outdoor or two-person shower · a freestanding soaking tub. A cabin carrying none of them medians $62,090. A cabin carrying three or more medians $93,658 — and the 17 cabins carrying two or more, out of 186, supply 9 of the top twenty.

The single strongest feature

A theater or cinema room. The 21 that have one median $83,277 against $65,245, they run 83.3% occupancy, and 8 of the top twenty have one against 2.3 expected if it were spread at random. It is also the only feature whose lift holds up inside every rate band (+14%, +22%, +22%), so it is not simply a marker of an expensive cabin.

The right tub is not the obvious one

A freestanding or soaking tub (21 cabins) medians $76,800. The jacuzzi or heart-shaped tub — the Smokies cliché — appears in 63 cabins and medians $69,093, barely above the market, and those cabins supply fewer top-twenty places than their share would predict. Same idea, two eras, very different results.

What to leave out

The family-cabin playbook actively costs money in this segment. Rustic / log-cabin styling $60,232 vs $68,036 (-11%). Sofa bed or bunk space $62,750 vs $67,576 (-7%). Pet friendly $58,763 vs $67,576 (-13%). An arcade or game room — which is worth real money in a larger group cabin — is worth nothing here: $68,038 against $66,121, and 6 top-twenty places against 7.8 expected.

The common thread

Every one of these is about two people staying in rather than a group going out. A theater room, a sauna, a soaking tub, a sound system and a private outdoor shower all make the cabin itself the destination for an evening. Bunk space, an arcade and a pet policy all say “bring more people” — and in this segment the market pays less for that, mostly through occupancy: pet-friendly cabins run 64.8% against 73.3% for the market.

Studios

16 of the 186 describe themselves as a studio rather than a separate bedroom.

Studios median $72,934 against $66,489 for the couples one-bedroom market as a whole — they are not a discount product here. The strongest studio in the county grossed $139,709 at $435 a night.

How this was measured

The sweep. 240 queries from 20 separate centres covering Gatlinburg, Pigeon Forge, Sevierville, Wears Valley, Pittman Center, Kodak, Seymour and the Douglas Lake and English Mountain edges returned 1,243 distinct listings, of which 1,172 are studios or one-bedrooms and 1,126 are entire places. Multiple centres are necessary because the data source ranks results by relevance to a single point, so one centre under-samples the edges of a 590-square-mile county.

The screen. Entire studios and one-bedrooms, sold to couples, sleeping two to four. The couples classification was read in context rather than pattern-matched — a keyword search alone matched a listing describing “a couple of dogs” on the property and several six- and seven-guest group cabins that mention a “romantic getaway or family vacation” in passing. Those were removed by hand. The top 260 candidates were measured; 186 qualify with a complete twelve-month record.

The arithmetic. Revenue is the sum of twelve individual monthly records. Nights sold are each month's occupancy applied to that month's own length; the nightly rate is total revenue divided by nights actually sold, and occupancy is those nights over 365 — so all three figures reconcile. The platform's own headline annual revenue field was not used: on these same cabins it ran high on 234 of 237, by a median of 15.7% and by as much as 36.6%.
